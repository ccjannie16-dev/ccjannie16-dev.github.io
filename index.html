Case study · UX / product design

AImpower: a dashboard for speech-language pathologists

AImpower.org is a nonprofit building speech AI that works for people who stutter. I designed the clinician side: the web app a speech-language pathologist (SLP) uses to set goals with clients and follow their progress between sessions.

Role
UX / product designer, research contributor
Timeline
Sep to Dec 2025
Team
Nonprofit product team, developers, community members
Tools
Figma, interviews, usability testing

The problem

Most speech technology is trained on fluent speech, so it works worst for the people who stutter. AImpower's client tool supports people during video calls and lets them log how meetings went. Their speech-language pathologists had no place to set goals with a client or see that data between sessions.

What I designed

  • SLP dashboard: caseload totals, clients who need attention, goal achievement over time, and the newest client reflections.
  • Clients overview: a table of every client with status, last session, last goal update and goal completion.
  • Create goal flow: three steps to set a goal, pick how it is measured, and review it before it goes to the client.
  • Insights: confidence, anxiety, open stuttering and goal trends, viewable by week, month or three months.

I turned interviews and community feedback into wireframes, built the high-fidelity screens in Figma, iterated from usability findings, and prepared handoff files for developers.

Design decisions

  • Open stuttering counts as progress. The goal library includes goals like "Stutter openly 5 times," and Insights maps the days a client stuttered openly on a calendar. Success means speaking with confidence, not sounding fluent.
  • Start from a library, not a blank box. SLPs pick from 20+ common therapist goals sorted into emotional, cognitive, utility and speech categories, or reuse a recent goal, then edit it.
  • Show the metric before choosing it. Each measurement type (progress bar, 1 to 5 rating, emoji scale, word cloud, voice note and more) shows a live preview, with suggested metrics for the goal.
  • Review on the client's screen. The last step previews the goal as the client will see it on their phone, so both sides of the product are designed together.
  • Zoom levels for time. Week, month and three-month views change the heat map from days to weeks, so a short dip does not hide long-term growth.

Case study · AI explainability

Next-Token Prediction Inspector

When I use ChatGPT I only see the finished answer. I made a web app that runs a real small language model in the browser and shows the choices it scores before it writes the next word.

Role
Concept, interaction design, prompt experiments
Timeline
Summer 2026, Cornell course project
Model
Xenova/distilgpt2 via Transformers.js
Built with
HTML, CSS, JavaScript, AI coding assistance
Try it in your browser ↗

Real outputs from the app · top 6 of 50,257 tokens

    The question

    A model does not write a sentence at once. For every step it gives a raw score, a logit, to each token in its vocabulary. I wanted people to see those scores, because a confident-looking answer can come from a close race between options.

    What I designed

    • Logits converted to probabilities with softmax over the full vocabulary, then shown as the top 8 bars so they are easy to compare.
    • The prompt split into tokens, with a dot marking a leading space, so people see how the model reads text.
    • Food, Science and Music context buttons, plus a confidence and spread readout, so the page invites comparison instead of a single result.

    Iteration

    The first version felt like a results page with no reason to try it twice. I added the context buttons and a comparison sentence, then retested the app myself after each change instead of assuming it still worked.

    Why it matters for product design

    Showing the margin between the top two guesses is a small pattern that could help people calibrate trust in AI output, and spot when an answer was close to going another way.

    After "concluded," the model gave "that" a 60.2% chance. Likely is not the same as certain.

    Case study · Game and narrative design

    Aftercolor

    A short narrative game about a home-care robot, Bibo, who has waited 347 days for a woman who will not come back. By finishing the ordinary acts of care she left behind, the player brings color back to a gray apartment.

    Role
    Game design, narrative, UX, art direction
    Timeline
    Summer 2026
    Scope
    5 chapters, 6 endings, mini-games, Gallery and story map
    Built with
    HTML, CSS, JavaScript; AI-assisted code, art and music
    Play in your browser ↗

    Design rule

    Memory does not disappear all at once. First the route becomes longer. Bibo still knows that someone mattered before it can find who she was, and its body keeps her habits after its mind loses the reason for them. Every system in the game follows that rule.

    Color as progress

    The apartment starts in grayscale. Color returns only when an act of care reaches the living world: cooking her recipe, finishing a puzzle, waiting on the balcony for the cat. Progress is something the player sees in the room, not a number on a bar.

    What I did

    • Wrote the story bible, branching choices and six endings, and mapped them into a flowchart players can revisit.
    • Designed the mini-games as care tasks, and set up an AI pipeline for code (Codex), illustration (Midjourney with reference locks for consistent characters) and music.
    • Ran playtests with my instructor and revised onboarding, skip controls and the Gallery from the feedback.

    "The tragedy is not that Bibo forgets Abby. It is that sometimes it has to search for her."
